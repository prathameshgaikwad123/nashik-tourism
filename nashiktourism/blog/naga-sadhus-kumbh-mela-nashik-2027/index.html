Naga Sadhus at Nashik Kumbh Mela 2027
Who Are They & How to Show Respect

Among the most striking figures at Kumbh Mela. A respectful introduction to who they are and how to behave around them.

Kumbh MelaApril 20269 min read

For most visitors, the first sight of a Naga Sadhu stops them completely. Ash-covered, armed with tridents, some with matted locks several feet long, moving in processions that part crowds like water — Naga Sadhus are the defining visual of Kumbh Mela. But who they actually are, and what their presence at Kumbh means, is far less understood than their appearance. This guide explains the basics — clearly and respectfully.

📅 Amrit Snan Dates and Processions

Naga Sadhus traditionally take part in the Amrit Snan processions. The Amrit Snan dates reported for 2027 are August 2, August 31, September 11 (Nashik) and September 12 (Trimbakeshwar) — reported, not yet confirmed. Which akharas bathe on which date, and the timings, routes and viewing arrangements for the processions, have not been announced in sources we could locate. Check live updates closer to the date.

Who Are Naga Sadhus?

Naga Sadhus are initiated ascetics — renunciates who have completely abandoned worldly life to pursue moksha (liberation). The word "Naga" is commonly said to come from Sanskrit meaning naked, referring to the traditional practice of living without clothing as a rejection of all worldly attachment and ego.

They belong to the Shaiva tradition (followers of Lord Shiva) and live in organised groups called Akharas — ancient monastic orders with a long history. Traditionally they were also warrior-monks, trained to protect temples and pilgrimage sites during invasions — which is why many still carry weapons like trishuls (tridents), swords and axes.

By tradition, a Naga Sadhu undergoes a long and rigorous initiation process that takes many years. The final initiation is described as a symbolic death and rebirth — the initiate performs their own funeral rites (pind daan), signifying that they have died to the world and are now beyond it. After initiation they renounce their birth name, family connections and all possessions.

What They Actually Practice — Daily Life

Away from Kumbh, Naga Sadhus traditionally live in the Himalayas, forests, or within their Akhara compounds. Their daily routine is described as involving:

  • Dhuni — a sacred fire that is never extinguished, around which they meditate, sleep and live
  • Vibhuti — sacred ash smeared all over the body, representing Shiva's nature and the impermanence of the physical form
  • Yoga and meditation — many hours daily, often in extreme postures
  • Rudraksha — beads of the Elaeocarpus seed worn as sacred garlands
  • Cannabis (Ganja) — in the tradition, associated with Shiva and used by some sadhus as a ritual substance. This is part of their own practice, not something visitors should assume is permitted for them
  • A simple diet — often a single meal a day, traditionally received as bhiksha (alms) from devotees

The Akharas at Nashik Kumbh Mela 2027

Akharas are traditionally grouped into Shaiva (worshippers of Shiva) and Vaishnava (worshippers of Vishnu) traditions. Media reports describe the Vaishnava akharas’ snan at Ramkund in Nashik on 11 September 2027 and the Shaiva akharas’ snan at Trimbakeshwar on 12 September 2027 (reported, not yet confirmed). The Amrit Snan Procession — What Actually Happens

The Amrit Snan procession is not a performance. It is a sacred ritual with a long tradition at Kumbh Melas. Timings, routes and the order of the akharas for 2027 have not been announced in sources we could locate, so what follows describes the traditional character of the procession, not a schedule.

Before the snan
Akhara camps prepare. Traditionally, sadhus perform rituals at the dhuni fire, apply vibhuti and dress for the procession within the camp.
The procession
Procession forms and departs. Traditionally the akharas move in an order of seniority, with senior saints at the front and Naga Sadhus marching on foot, to the sound of drums, conches and chanting.
The bath
Sacred bath. By tradition each akhara enters the ghat in turn, with senior saints bathing first, and the procession continues until all the akharas have completed their snan.
Afterwards
Public bathing. Traditionally the akharas bathe first. Whether, when and where the ghats will open to the public on Amrit Snan days has not been announced.

How to Respectfully Witness Naga Sadhus at Nashik Kumbh

Naga Sadhus are not a tourist attraction. They are serious ascetics who have dedicated their entire existence to spiritual practice. Approaching them with respect is essential — and practically speaking, approaching them disrespectfully is also unwise.

  • Do not touch a Naga Sadhu without their clear invitation to do so
  • Ask before photographing — many senior sadhus do not permit photographs. Always ask with folded hands. Some do allow it; many do not.
  • Do not offer money directly — this is often considered disrespectful. Offerings of fruit, flowers or sacred items are more appropriate if you wish to offer something.
  • Watch from a respectful distance during the procession — do not try to push to the front
  • Do not call out or shout at Naga Sadhus in the procession to get their attention
  • Receive blessings correctly — if a Sadhu offers a blessing, bow your head and receive it with both hands or folded hands
📷 Photography Guidance

Procession routes, viewing areas and any restrictions on photography or access have not been announced. Whatever the arrangements, expect very dense crowds: arrive early, follow the instructions of police and volunteers, and do not climb onto structures or private property to get a better view.

Where Are the Akhara Camps in Nashik?

At Kumbh Melas, the akharas traditionally set up large camps, and the temporary township for sadhus is often called a Sadhugram. Where the camps will be at Nashik and Trimbakeshwar, who allocates the land, and what facilities they will have has not been announced in sources we could locate.

Akhara camps are traditionally places of devotion, with incense, dhuni fires and kirtan, and some have welcomed visitors and served prasad at past gatherings. Whether the camps at Nashik will be open to visitors, and on what terms, has not been announced, so do not assume access. If you are admitted, interact respectfully with the sadhus and follow the instructions of the camp.

The Difference at Nashik — Shaiva vs Vaishnava

For the third Amrit Snan, media reports describe the Shaiva and Vaishnava akharas bathing at different locations on different days (reported, not yet confirmed):

  • Shaiva akharas (the tradition to which Naga Sadhus are most commonly linked) — reported at Kushavarta in Trimbakeshwar on 12 September 2027
  • Vaishnava akharas (Bairagis) — reported at Ramkund in Nashik city on 11 September 2027

Those hoping to see the ash-covered Naga Sadhus should note that the reported Trimbakeshwar date is September 12, 2027. Vaishnava sadhus, reported for Nashik on September 11, are generally clothed, which makes for a different sight. Which akharas bathe on the reported August dates has not been confirmed, so check the latest notices before planning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can anyone become a Naga Sadhu?

Traditionally yes, though the process is described as extremely rigorous. It involves years of preparation under a guru, tests of austerity, and the willingness to renounce all worldly attachments permanently. Some akharas are also said to initiate women.

Are Naga Sadhus dangerous?

Naga Sadhus are ascetics focused on spiritual practice, but they should not be provoked, disrespected or treated as entertainment. As at any very crowded event, follow the instructions of police and volunteers; we cannot give safety guarantees.

Do Naga Sadhus always live naked?

Traditionally, many Shaiva Naga Sadhus live without clothing as a sign of complete renunciation. Practice varies, and some wear minimal cloth or basic coverings depending on the setting or season. Vaishnava Digambar sadhus are described as following a similar practice.

What do Naga Sadhus eat?

Traditionally they subsist largely on bhiksha — food given by devotees — often one meal a day, and the diet is described as simple. Some sadhus use cannabis as a ritual substance associated with Lord Shiva; this is part of their own tradition and not something visitors should assume is permitted for them.
